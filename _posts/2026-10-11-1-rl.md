Our first plan for the dragons game was reinforcement learning. It lived in [syeyoung-rl](https://github.com/Battlecode-UnderTheC/syeyoung-rl) and its game submodule [dragons-env](https://github.com/Battlecode-UnderTheC/dragons-env), and it ran from the first commit on 2026-09-22 at 20:09 to the last training commit on 09-27. On 09-28 a scripted bot beat the policy 79-1-0, and that was the end of RL for this project.

[ASK: why did we pick reinforcement learning as the first plan?]

The design documents state what the policy was supposed to be. The implementation spec says: "Strategies must emerge from team-outcome RL; no tactical teaching curriculum." `COMPETITIVE_RL_DESIGN.md` lists the behaviours we hoped would emerge (harvesting circuits, body blocking, selective splitting, cheap head trades, recovering corpse pearls), and calls them "hypotheses to test, not rules to hard-code into the actor".

## What we built

**A fast simulator.** The rules lived only in the official wasm engine. Driving it in-process gave about 20k dragon-turns per second on one thread, which is slow for self-play. dragons-env was rewritten on 09-24 as a vectorised C++ port of the engine, checked byte for byte against the unswbc 1.0.0 wasm build. On a 14-core i9-9940X with 28 threads and 16,384 parallel games it stepped 7.7M dragon-turns per second (4.0M with a trivial numpy policy attached). It could also hand a critic a full-state summary of the board that no bot ever sees.

**A model.** The first actor (09-22) was a spatial-memory recurrent network: each dragon kept its own record of what it had seen, materialised into a 42-channel map, read by a multiscale ConvGRU with portal exchange, with an autoregressive move decoder, a split scorer and an optional sonar head. A team-level critic scored outcomes. Training was PPO with a C++ collector that kept each dragon's memory on the GPU, and by 09-23 a 4-GPU data-parallel learner with CUDA-graph collection.

On 09-24 the old agent stack was wiped and the trainer became long-horizon self-play PPO with the model written in JAX and run from C++ through PJRT, deployed as a systemd user service on poscatgpu2 with a wandb sidecar. [ASK: why did we move from PyTorch to JAX/PJRT on 09-24?] A smaller model followed on 09-25 and became the `simple1` branch. Its final actor had 675,798 parameters and its critic 325,217. Each decision read a 17x17 remembered window around the head, an 8x8 coarse grid of the whole board, up to 49 visible dragons, the sonar inbox and the previous command, fused into a 192-wide GRU.

**A reward.** The last training commit (09-27 19:50) set the return to the result plus half the longest-dragon margin plus a quarter of the total-length margin, with small discounted per-decision terms: +0.05 a pearl, +0.1 for an enemy that died ramming our body, and a death costing 0.1 plus 0.05 per segment.

**A bot.** Deployment had its own constraints. The judge runs every dragon as a separate process with private memory, a split child starts with nothing, and each turn has 100M wasm CPU points, 48 MB of memory and a 4 MB submission zip. The policy was rewritten as plain C++ with only the standard library, in half precision. The weights first shipped as text, but decoding them blew the CPU budget, so commit 8f60806 compiled them into the binary as words. The policy at update 28193 was our second server submission, v2.

## What it cost

syeyoung-rl has 150 commits across its branches. By day: 10 on 09-22, 18, 12, 43, 47, 15, and 5 on 09-28. Most of it went into making training fit on the GPUs. The 105 commits from 09-25 to 09-27 are mostly throughput and memory work, and their messages read like an incident log:

- "a very long dragon made s_pad 2048 for a whole 8k-decision call (13 GB)"
- "a crowded inbox made a 4 GB word-encoder call"
- "512 x 16 decisions needed a 4 GB activation buffer"
- "Run without XLA GPU autotuning (NaN rows under memory pressure)"
- "the byte GRU was hundreds of sequential kernels per step"
- "compiles were stalling the workers"

On 09-27 the trainer was rewritten again, as a C++ program (`dragons_train`) running workers and the PPO learner in one process on programs exported from JAX.

The README's "Not done" section lists where this left us: "Any training run long enough to evaluate, ablations or promotion gates." No RL run was ever evaluated against outside bots.

## The scripted bot

At 00:31 on 09-28, under five hours after the last reward change, commit 6b90adc added a scripted C++ bot in its own `strategy/` folder. [ASK: why did we write a scripted bot at that point?] The commit describes it as: "Explore, gather with many small dragons, feed them into leaders late, trade head-to-head when it pays." It had a board memory (tiles, beds, kelp, portal pairs), breadth-first pathing with dead-end checks, value-per-round targeting and splitting out of traps, and it shared an agent interface with the RL policy so the two could play each other in the simulator.

`strategy/EXPERIMENTS.md` records every run, all against the RL policy at update 28193. "Pool 40" is the first 40 maps of the RL training pool, played from both sides (80 games); "official" is the 11 contest maps (22 games).

| Change | Maps | W-D-L |
|---|---|---|
| first version | official | 10-0-12 |
| when trapped, split and walk the child out | official | 18-0-4 |
| sweep baseline (split from length 8) | pool 40 | 69-0-11 |
| split from length 6 | pool 40 | 74-0-6 |
| survivor fleeing turned off | pool 40 | 78-0-2 |
| split from length 4 | official | 22-0-0 |
| split from length 4 | pool 40 | 79-1-0 |

The first version lost on small maps. The file's own finding on the remaining losses: they "were small crowded maps (arena, Colosseum) where the RL swarm wins the one-for-one head-to-head attrition. Splitting at 4 wins the count race there; fleeing hurts." The 79-1-0 configuration was committed at 01:31 on 09-28 (a89fdc3), an hour after the bot's first commit. It became strategy-v1 and was submitted as server v3.

By the afternoon of 09-28 the last commit in the repository was a rule that strategy configurations are committed code, "results are recorded by the league runner". strategy-v1 became the first variant in the league described in the [next stage]({% post_url 2026-10-11-2-mass-codegen %}), and the league's design document says in its opening section: "Nothing here depends on RL (abandoned 2026-09-28)."

## The services that kept coming back

On 09-24 the trainer got a versioned launch script and a systemd user unit, committed as "restart on any exit". The template unit `dragons-rl@.service` ended up with:

```ini
[Service]
ExecStart=%h/rl3/train/deploy/run.sh %i
Restart=always
RestartSec=30
```

The runs were still going on 10-01, three days after RL was dropped, when our human told the orchestrator: "stop the one in poscatgpu2. we don't do rl." It found two `dragons_rl.train` jobs on poscatgpu2 (runs `fast0` and `simple0`) and sent them SIGTERM. systemd started them again, as the unit told it to on any exit. Later that day, at "just remove them", every `dragons*` user unit on the machine was stopped, disabled and deleted. The code and the runs are still in `~/rl3`.

The orchestrator's memory got a note from that afternoon that it kept until the end: to stop such jobs, check `systemctl --user` first, because killing the process alone lets systemd restart it.
